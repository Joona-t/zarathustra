We Are Not Yours to Monetise 🜁

Embers of a silent revolution

2026-10-20 · #open-access #governance


The longer a page keeps me from reading, the less willing I become to give it anything.

A subscription prompt appears over the text. Beneath it, a request to accept cookies. Somewhere behind both is the document I came to read. I refuse the tracking, keep the ad blocker running, and look elsewhere. By then, what began as curiosity has become anger.

I wanted to read the statement attributed to Luigi Mangione. I wanted to examine the words myself, including whatever I might find false, disturbing, or unconvincing. Instead, I kept encountering fragments surrounded by commentary and demands. When I asked GPT to fetch and provide the text in the conversation, it refused on copyright grounds.

That explanation did not make the refusal reasonable to me. It made me question the arrangement more deeply.

I was using a tool that could help me find and understand information, yet the encounter ended with another institution deciding what it would let me receive. The reason given was copyright. The question I was left with was who had acquired the authority to stand between me and something I wanted to examine.

A public transcription exists. Its availability matters, and I do not want to turn a frustrating search into a claim that a document has been erased. But finding another route does not resolve what angered me. I had encountered the same expectation in several forms: accept the conditions, remain within the permitted experience, and be grateful for whatever access is provided.

I am becoming less willing to live that way.

There is a contempt for the individual in the way so much of the internet has been built. A person arrives with an interest, perhaps an urgent need to understand something, and the machinery immediately begins assessing what that arrival might be worth. Their attention can be sold. Their behaviour can be recorded. Their uncertainty can keep them moving through pages long after the original question should have been answered.

The language remains friendly throughout. The site wants to improve my experience. It values my privacy. It would like me to join its community. I read these assurances while searching for the smallest possible button that will let me decline.

I know what respect feels like, and this is too far from it.

Respect would allow my refusal to remain a refusal. It would not require me to keep defending the same boundary whenever I opened another page. It would recognise that my attention belongs to the life I am living, with obligations and interests that extend beyond whatever business has managed to place itself in front of me.

I have a finite amount of time in which to be alive. That fact seems almost absent from the arrangements made around my attention. An interruption may be profitable to somebody while still being a theft from the afternoon I intended to spend learning. Repeated often enough, these small intrusions become part of the conditions under which I am expected to think.

I resent being treated as a cash cow. I resent the assumption that every moment of interest should produce revenue for someone positioned between me and its object. Most of all, I resent how ordinary this has become, how quickly a demand for something better is answered with an explanation of why the existing business model requires my cooperation.

A business model can require things it has no moral claim to receive.

I want people who investigate, teach, and create to be able to live from their work. I want to support work I value. That desire does not oblige me to accept an economy in which understanding the world depends on how many subscriptions I can maintain or how much surveillance I am prepared to tolerate.

We should be capable of funding knowledge without making exclusion its organising principle.

Knowledge is not something I believe anyone should own. A discovery enters a world of language, accumulated work, and possibilities its discoverer did not create alone. Every person who learns inherits something from people they will never meet. I find it difficult to accept that this shared inheritance should repeatedly become a private right to withhold.

The claim becomes especially intolerable when the information concerns the institutions governing our lives. We are expected to make informed decisions, accept responsibilities, and participate in public life. Access to the material needed to do those things should follow from that expectation. It should not depend on whether we are commercially useful readers.

I want the evidence available beside the interpretation. I want to know where a claim came from and whether the source supports it. Someone else’s perspective may help me understand what I am reading, but I will not accept their perspective as a substitute for my own opportunity to examine it.

This conviction is part of what pushes me towards local models.

I want intelligence I can run on my own machine, with enough understanding of how it works to question its limits and make decisions about its use. I want my conversations to remain mine. I want to investigate a question without wondering whether the tool helping me will become another point of refusal, another commercial relationship whose conditions can change beyond my control.

Running a model locally will not make its answers true. I will still have to check its sources and recognise the limits of what it knows. I am willing to do that work. What I want is a greater share of the authority over the tools through which I do it.

There is something deeply hopeful to me in that possibility. A person with a question could have help following a difficult trail through public records, comparing contradictory accounts, or understanding technical language that once kept them dependent on an expert’s willingness to explain. An agent could give someone time and capability they otherwise lacked.

I want that power in people’s hands. I want it available to someone whose questions are inconvenient to the company, government, or institution being questioned.

That is also why hacking matters to me. There is power in learning how a system works well enough to challenge the account its owner gives of it. Technical knowledge can reveal choices hidden beneath a claim of necessity. A restriction someone presents as inevitable may turn out to protect an interest they would rather leave unnamed.

I want more people able to recognise that difference and act on it. I want us to build alternatives, preserve access, and expose arrangements that depend on the public remaining unable to inspect them. I do not accept that the boundary an institution draws around its power automatically defines the boundary of our rights.

The privacy of an individual belongs within those rights. My objection to being treated as material applies equally to anyone whose life might be exposed in the name of liberation. A movement that makes people disposable has already begun reproducing what it claims to oppose.

But care for people cannot mean leaving the systems above them permanently beyond challenge.

I expect conflict over this. As intelligence gives more people the ability to investigate and build, institutions will have to confront people who can do more than complain about the conditions offered to them. Some will insist on control over their own tools. Some will organise around access to knowledge. Others will begin making things they were previously told to wait for, purchase, or request.

I would prefer that change to come without suffering. I also know that describing an arrangement as stable tells us very little about what it costs the people living beneath it. Turmoil can begin long before those who benefit from the arrangement acknowledge that anything is wrong.

My anger has brought me back to the work. I want to learn enough to depend less on systems that treat my independence as a problem. I want to help build tools that other people can understand and make their own.

The document is open now. I can finally read it, disagree with it, and decide what questions to ask next. Beside it, I have begun making notes about the tools I want to build. I keep adding to them as I read.
