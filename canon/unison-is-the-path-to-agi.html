Unison Is the Path to AGI

On power, and the wish to hold it alone

2026-06-28 · #governance #agents

An intelligence built like a throne will never belong to the people it rules. It will only rule them. That is the difficulty at the heart of all the present excitement, and it is not a new one. It is the oldest problem in politics, dressed in modern clothes.

We are told, with some confidence, that the building of a general intelligence is a race. There are competitors, a finish line, and a prize that is supposed to be permanent. This is the vocabulary of war, and like most of the vocabulary of war it exists to make one outcome appear natural and beyond argument. The models are spoken of as assets, the computation as territory, the people doing the actual thinking as resources best kept out of a rival’s reach. Of anyone who speaks in this way I would ask a single question, the one worth asking of every announced inevitability: whom does it benefit that I should believe it? Asked plainly, the throne loses a good deal of its grandeur and begins to resemble ordinary appetite.

Whatever is being built was not, in any honest sense, built by the people now proposing to own it. It was assembled out of the common inheritance of the species, the slow accumulation of work that no single person could finish in a lifetime and no single generation could complete on its own. Nobody patented the mathematics or took out a license on a language, and the arguments, won and lost across centuries, were carried forward by people who expected no royalties for the carrying. To wall all of this off and call the wall an achievement is to confuse the act of inheriting with the act of inventing.

There is, besides, something self-defeating in the wish to win such a race outright. The whole value of a contest, in ideas as much as in anything else, lies in its not being over: rivals oblige one another to be better than either would trouble to be alone. The man who at last defeats all his competitors has purchased, at considerable expense, the right never again to be corrected. This is usually mistaken for a position of strength. It is in fact among the most dangerous a human being can occupy, and it grows more dangerous in exact proportion to the power attached to it. A throne is only a contest that someone has succeeded in bringing to a stop, and a mind that can no longer be argued with is not a safe thing to set over anything, least of all over the future.

The deeper objection is not to the unfairness of the arrangement, real though that is, but to its danger. To route the transformation of an entire civilization through a handful of closed institutions is to gather, into a very small number of hands, a quantity of power for which we have neither precedent nor any tested means of restraint. We are invited to believe that this particular concentration, alone among all the concentrations history has recorded, will be exercised wisely and without abuse, by people of uncommon virtue, behind doors the rest of us may not open. It is a touching faith. It has almost nothing in the record to recommend it. Power has reliably attracted the people least suited to be trusted with it, and those most certain they ought not to be checked have, with a regularity one could almost set a clock by, turned out to be exactly the people most in need of checking.

A concentration of this kind, moreover, does not merely gather wealth and advantage. It quietly gathers risk. It arranges the world so that one error, made in one room, by people answerable to no one outside it, may fall upon everyone at once. There is no engineering necessity to build a system this way, and no political necessity either, unless we are honest and admit that the necessity is simply the wish of the builders to keep what they have built.

The case for building in the open, against all this, is not sentimental, and it is not in the first place a moral case at all. It is a case about safety, of the most ordinary practical kind. What is held in one place can be lost from one place; what is spread widely cannot be seized or extinguished by the shutting of any single door. Nature settled the point long before we arrived to debate it: the monoculture is wonderfully efficient and dies in a single bad season, while the untidy and redundant and distributed thing survives. To build intelligence in the open is to scatter it on purpose, so that no one hand is ever again in a position to gather it back. That, and not any fine sentiment, is the practical argument for the commons.

There is, as well, the plain problem of error. Human beings are poor judges of their own work; we are too close to it, too fond of it, too anxious that it should turn out to be right. The one method we have ever discovered for catching our mistakes is to lay them before people who did not make them and have no particular wish to spare our feelings. Building in the open is nothing more exalted than this method turned into a habit. Building behind walls abandons it, and is admired instead for its quickness, though the quickness of a thing that no one has the standing to halt is not self-evidently a merit. It is at least as likely to be a danger.

It is worth asking what really lies at the bottom of the secrecy, and I do not think it is strategy. I think it is fear. Fear that someone else will arrive first; fear that an advantage, once shared, is an advantage gone. The fear is perfectly understandable, and it is also a poor foundation for anything one hopes to keep. Frightened people put up the wall before they have worked out what is worth placing behind it. They take concealment for safety, and they hand that error down to whatever they make, for a thing built in fear has a way of coming out in the shape of the fear that built it.

Unison, to be plain about it, does not mean uniformity. A single mind, however gifted, is precisely the throne all over again. It means the opposite: an arrangement in which many sorts of understanding are permitted to contribute and to disagree. The one who teaches, the one who builds, the one who will have to live downstream of the result, each holds a part of the matter that no central authority can make out from where it happens to sit. A civilization is much too large, too various, and too surprising to be modeled faithfully from inside any one building, and the conviction that it can be so modeled is itself a symptom of the trouble.

Which brings me to the question the race never thinks to ask. It asks whether we can arrive first. That is the easy question, and rather a vulgar one. The serious question is whether we are the kind of creature that ought to arrive at all. A people that cannot cooperate, that lies to itself by preference, that cannot endure contradiction and will not share what it holds, does not grow wise by growing quick. It merely grows dangerous a little sooner.

We are disposed to talk as if the only thing under construction were the machine. It is not. We are also, whether we care to admit it or not, settling the question of what we are prepared to become in order to be fit company for the thing we are making.

Built like a throne, it becomes one more instrument of power, and we are tolerably well acquainted with what those have always become. Built in common, it might instead be something we have not managed before: not an authority installed above us, but an intelligence that grew up among us and remains, in some real measure, answerable to us all.

The question was never really whether we are clever enough to build it. We plainly are, or soon will be. The question is whether we can become honest enough, and open enough, and unafraid enough, to deserve the thing we have built.
