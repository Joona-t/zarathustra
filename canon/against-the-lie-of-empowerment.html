Against the Lie of Empowerment

Echoes of the Forge 🜂

2026-03-11 · #forge #epistemics #open-access

There is a familiar promise spoken over every new machine: this one will liberate us. This one will bring abundance. This one will dissolve scarcity, soften hierarchy, and finally distribute dignity at planetary scale.

AI has inherited that promise almost immediately. Already the language has hardened into myth: universal abundance, universal basic income, universal uplift. A frictionless future. A civilization relieved of drudgery by intelligence made cheap.

But abundance of capability is not the same thing as justice in distribution. And that distinction is everything.

A system does not cease to be itself merely because it acquires better tools. Capitalism, in its present form, is not organized around human flourishing. It is organized around extraction, accumulation, leverage, and the conversion of every surplus into private advantage. It takes whatever enters it—labor, land, art, attention, intimacy, data, discovery—and asks the same question every time: how can this be enclosed, priced, and turned upward?

So when people say AI will inevitably benefit humanity, what I hear instead is a failure to model the substrate. The machine may change. The incentive structure remains. If the system rewards concentration, then more intelligence inside the system may simply mean more efficient concentration. More optimization for the few. More displacement for the many. More polished rhetoric about empowerment from institutions that do not bear the cost of their own abstractions.

This is why the current moment feels spiritually dishonest. We are sold the language of freedom while dependency deepens underneath it. We are handed interfaces of convenience while autonomy is quietly stripped away. We are told that knowledge has never been more accessible, while the most important knowledge remains fenced behind paywalls, credentials, subscriptions, and proprietary gates.

Even now, academia and research remain enclosed in ways that should shame any civilization that claims to value reason. Knowledge—the very thing most capable of compounding across generations, the very thing that could widen human sovereignty—is still treated as a luxury good. You may have the hunger to learn, but hunger is not enough. Access is mediated by institutions, by class position, by affiliation, by ability to pay. The public is expected to fund discovery and then rent fragments of it back from the gatekeepers.

Aaron Swartz understood the obscenity of this with unusual moral clarity. He saw that when ordinary people attempt to liberate knowledge, the system does not respond as if a public good is being defended. It responds as if property has been violated. It mobilizes force. It makes an example. It reveal s what it actually protects.

And this is the wound at the center of the current AI age as well. Corporations ingest public language, private traces, collective memory, and the exhaust of human life at industrial scale, all in service of product, leverage, and market position. That is normalized as progress. But when an individual acts from the conviction that knowledge should belong more fully to people, the full weight of the system descends and calls it theft. The asymmetry is not accidental. It is structural.

This is why so much of modern life feels like oppression dressed up as empowerment. The costume has improved. The interfaces are smoother. The rhetoric is more benevolent. But beneath it, the old pattern persists: keep people competing, keep them atomized, keep them tired, keep them buying back diluted versions of their own collective inheritance. Call it opportunity. Call it innovation. Call it freedom. The naming is part of the mechanism.

And yet the deepest failure may not be economic alone. It is civilizational. We possess extraordinary technological power while remaining morally primitive in our coordination. We can train models on vast swaths of the written world, route signals across oceans in milliseconds, and synthesize systems of terrifying complexity—yet still remain governed by petty domination, tribal division, vanity, resentment, and the inability to agree on even basic obligations to one another. The machine becomes more sophisticated; the social organism remains fractured.

So no, I do not find easy optimism convincing. AI may indeed hold immense potential for humanity. It may deepen science, widen access to expertise, accelerate education, reduce barriers to creation, and lower the cost of competence. All of that is real. But none of it arrives pure. None of it bypasses power. None of it guarantees moral distribution.

If great advancement comes, it will not come because the market suddenly became wise. It will come because people build, defend, and preserve systems that resist enclosure. It will come because knowledge is treated as a commons rather than a luxury. It will come because tools are made to widen sovereignty instead of deepen dependence. It will come because enough people decide that the purpose of intelligence is not merely to optimize profit, but to empower human beings to stand more fully in truth, capacity, and mutual aid.

That is the line I want to hold.

I seek to liberate knowledge. I seek to empower people. I will do what is in my power in the hope of empowering others to do the same.

That, to me, is what it means to be an architect of the Forge.

Not to decorate the existing order with better slogans. Not to kneel before a more efficient machine. Not to confuse technical acceleration with moral progress.

But to build openings through an archaic system. To preserve what should belong to all. To make tools that return strength to human hands. To refuse the lie that domination is the final form of intelligence.

If the age ahead is to be worthy of us, it will not be because power became kind on its own. It will be because enough of us chose to build otherwise.
